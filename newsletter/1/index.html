Roundtable-1 Roundup

Roundtable-1 convened transit planners, academics, operators and builders to discuss opening up India's public-transport data. The conversation covered what's working, what's broken, and what needs to happen next — from vendor lock-in and data quality to procurement reform and national policy.

Key outcomes

Consensus: open data and open systems are essential for public transport in India — proven in Delhi and via Namma Yatri.

Barriers: data locked in proprietary vendor systems, unwillingness to share, poor data quality (60% of project time), weak institutional capacity.

What's needed: national policy mandates, capacity building for agencies, standardized procurement, open-source platform adoption.

Where the discussion converged

No formal votes were taken — what follows is where the room's discussion converged rather than binding decisions.

Public ownership of transit data

Taxpayer-funded data should be publicly owned — monetization models can still exist on top.

Open systems over proprietary

Build on open-source platforms to avoid vendor lock-in and enable third-party innovation.

Data exchange architecture

Use data exchanges as an intermediate layer to separate data from applications.

Current state of open mobility systems

What's working

  • Platforms exist: IUDX and Transport Stack are built and proven functional.
  • Expertise available: IISc, IIIT Delhi, IIT Kanpur — on both technology and policy.
  • Success cases: Transport for All challenge — startups built solid solutions for Belgavi, Pune, Devangiri on limited data.
  • Government interest: MoHUA and Smart City Mission engaged; some officials recognize value.

What's not working

  • Data locked in vendor systems: vendors have no incentive to open up; it takes years to force.
  • Unwillingness to share: cities fear exposing service gaps and want to control customer-facing apps.
  • Poor data quality: 60% of project time fixing it; 37% of trips break route IDs and trip IDs don't change properly.
  • No data SLAs: feeds shut off arbitrarily despite heavy citizen use; no guarantees.
  • Privacy paralysis: DPDP uncertainty over what's shareable leads to inaction.
  • Operators seen as competitors: cities withhold bus stop locations, missing collaborative benefits.
  • Official churn: frequent transfers erase institutional memory and restart relationships.

Data ownership and legal framework

Ownership clarity

  • Transit data is a public good: taxpayer-funded data should be taxpayer-owned — not government or vendor-owned.
  • Namma Yatri model: a private entity opened its data for trust, enabling research and city planning in Chennai and Bangalore.

Legal and policy context

  • Personal data protected: DPDP Act (mandatory 2027) needs consent for PII — specific, revocable, legislatively compliant.
  • Non-personal data unregulated: no ownership concept — a "free for all" with no sharing mandate.
  • Geospatial exception: National Geospatial Policy (2022) mandates publicly-funded geospatial data be public; monetization allowed.
  • No near-term change: non-personal framework unlikely to be revisited for a decade after the 6–8 year DPDP process.

Scaling challenges and solutions

Why national scale-up fails

India's diversity

Every state/city is different — reaching each SDU takes far longer; scaling won't happen in the first meeting.

No central mandate

Unlike roads/flyovers (funded despite <10% car ownership), open data has no enforcement as law/rule.

Capacity gaps

Agencies can't code or maintain data; each reinvents the wheel without standard formats.

Broken procurement

Tendering blocks innovation; qualification criteria loop — marketplace-based procurement is needed.

Proposed solutions

National level

  • Central alignment: if MoHUA or central entities set standards, everyone follows — fastest path to scale.
  • Policy → law: move from guidance to enforceable acts/rules with a national champion.
  • ONDC model: open network working — 9+ metros and bus entities joining; the future for distributed systems.

Institutional level

  • Capacity programs: standard formats beyond GTFS — crew, bus, depot management as a prequel to GTFS.
  • Handhold agencies: prove value via use cases — e.g. Chennai spotting unpaid commercial taxes through data overlays.
  • Tender specs: simple, mandatory clauses per SDU procurement ensuring data ownership and openness.

Technical level

  • Operational data standards: research India-specific standards for crew/fleet/depot data fitting local conditions.
  • Software escrow: UK-style escrow keeps continuity when vendors change — avoids 12–18 month re-stabilization.
  • Open-source community: vendors build on a shared base, adding value while staying compatible for the next vendor.

Data quality and standards

Quality issues identified

  • Route ID problems: 37% of trips don't follow route IDs — buses appear to continue the wrong trip.
  • Research impact: quality limits research; much time goes to quantifying it before use.
  • Metrics needed: the platform should record and publish quality metrics alongside the data.

Standardization gaps

  • AIS-140 GPS standards: present at policy level, but implementation varies.
  • ASRTU rate contracts: vendors chase profitable e-ticketing; promised apps and citizen features undelivered.
  • GTFS insufficient: needs an operational data layer (crew, depot, fleet) as a foundation beneath it.

Open questions

  • Revenue sharing: how do we compensate individuals whose aggregated data creates value, like social-media creators?
  • Joint ownership: should data individuals generate on public systems be jointly owned with the operator?
  • Vendor continuity: how do we balance open systems with vendor models when interoperability erodes lock-in?

What we're taking forward

1
Research consortium

Prof. Gita, Prof. Pravesh and Prof. Inder to build India-specific operational data standards (crew, fleet, depot).

2
Capacity-building curriculum

Train transit agencies on data-coding standards and maintenance.

3
Standard tender template

Mandatory tender clauses ensuring data ownership and openness for all SDU procurements.

4
Data-quality metrics

Record and publish quality metrics on the Delhi Transport Stack platform.

What's on Transport Stack

A look at the platform's features, the data already published, and what's being built on top of it.

Platform features

Multimodal coverage

Buses, metros and shared mobility (autos, e-rickshaws near metro stations) — all in a standardized format in one place, so you don't piece modes together manually.

Open-source code base

Journey planner, ETA services, bus-bunching detection and platform infrastructure — all available as public repositories anyone can read, fork and run.

Low entry barrier

A transparent, open ecosystem designed to cut the cost of entry for developers, researchers and small organizations building on transit data.

OTD migration in progress

The older Open Transit Data platform is being deprecated; all data is moving to Transport Stack with better consistency and a single source of truth.

Representative datasets on the platform

A snapshot of what's published today — the list keeps growing as more modes and cities come online:

Bus static data (GTFS)

Routes, stops, schedules and trip definitions for Delhi's bus network in standard GTFS format.

Bus real-time data (AVLS)

Live vehicle positions streamed from on-board GPS units — the basis for arrival predictions and bunching detection.

Bus ETA & derived feeds

Estimated time of arrival per stop, plus processed trip/route information and bus-bunching signals derived from raw AVLS.

Metro data

Metro routes, stations and schedules in the same standardized format as the bus feeds — so a single journey planner covers both.

Intermediate Public Transport

Shared-mobility data around metro stations — autos and e-rickshaws — currently collected via app, not as live operator feeds.

Journey planner APIs

Public API endpoints that wrap the underlying datasets so third-party apps can plan multimodal trips without re-implementing routing.

Coming soon: ticketing & ridership. Delhi's ticketing data exists but sits in a disguised internal system; transport-department approval is pending before it can be released for flow and ridership analysis on the platform.

Built on the stack

The point of an open data platform is what people build on top of it. Here's a featured build — with space for many more.

Talking Box — voice assistant for bus stops

Built by Chartr · live in Delhi
  • Press. Talk. Done. — one-button interaction; no smartphone, no internet needed by the rider.
  • Multilingual: Hindi & English today, more languages coming.
  • Real-time ETA: live arrivals, journey planning and route guidance spoken aloud.
  • Static fallback: uses scheduled APIs when real-time buses are unavailable; suggests alternative stops.
  • Powered by Chartr's live GTFS + AVLS data flowing through the Transport Stack.
